# All the Foundry Geo features you didn’t know existed

*Draft 2. Tag: Geo.*

Foundry handles geo through three different modelling approaches, and each one has an A side, the easy one, and a B side, the one that gives you more to play with. The first, the simplest, is the vanilla one: giving an object a geographic property, either latitude and longitude or a GeoJSON. With that you can draw its address, or the shape of a region, a district, a country or a continent. In the [documentation](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/geospatial/ontology/) they are called geopoint and geoshape, and objects that have them are indexed for geospatial search. That is the A side. The B side is giving it a twist and making the association dynamic: instead of a fixed property, you create geo objects with richer relationships to yours and you can have several over time, so its shape or its address keeps changing. Vanilla, but a little bit spicier.

The second approach is geotemporal series: attaching a track to an object, that is, points in space and time. Think of a delivery truck with a GPS reporting its position every few seconds. Those points don’t live in an ordinary property but in a special structure linked to the object, and the [documentation](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/geospatial/geotemporal-series-overview/) (which currently marks it as beta) describes a sync that indexes the data into an optimised database and an object type that references it. That is the A side. The B side is what lies underneath, which has a lot more depth than it looks. In our experience, how you configure the series depends on what you are going to ask of it: finding which tracks cross a polygon in a time window is not the same as asking where this truck was at five in the afternoon, and with many objects you notice. Underneath there is serious mathematics: we were lucky enough to meet at Palantir the person who built the geo engine, and the technology underneath is, at heart, their doctoral thesis, work on optimising mathematical curves to represent a many-dimensional space in a single dimension. It is delicate ground and it has changed a lot. We started when you had to configure it by hand with a YAML specification file, and today it is far better integrated, with [data modelling](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/geospatial/data-modeling/) and [integration with the ontology](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/geospatial/integrate-geotemporal-series-with-the-ontology/) much better described.

The third modelling route is the geohash, and it will probably ring a bell if you come from doing geo operations in big data. The A side looks like the same thing as the first approach: attaching to the object, to your truck, a property that works like a GeoJSON, only here it is an H3 index. The B side is what you can do underneath with PySpark, which is far more effective and far more interesting, above all for geospatial joins over large volumes of data. Geo operations are very expensive: comparing distances scales badly with large volumes, and you have to change your mindset so that geo comparisons become problems that parallelise as much as possible, built on spatial indexes. That is where tessellating space comes in. Uber developed [H3](https://h3geo.org/), which indexes space into hexagons of different sizes, and it is the one Palantir has adopted fairly natively: Pipeline Builder includes [geospatial transforms](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/pipeline-builder/transforms-geospatial/) with H3, such as covering a polygon with hexagons or getting a hexagon’s neighbours. You may already do it another way, with ESRI, with [Apache Sedona](https://sedona.apache.org/) or other big-data geo libraries, or even with geographic embeddings, and that can stay. But when we need a fast spatial join, or a use case that checks whether several things are in the same place at the same time, H3 has given us better performance. The idea we take to every project is to think of geo problems as big-data grouping problems: a group by, a window, an operation that can be parallelised over a geospatial index. With that in mind, we start working with you.
