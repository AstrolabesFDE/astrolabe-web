# All the Foundry Geo features you didn’t know existed

*Draft 1. Tag: Geo.*

When people talk about geo in Foundry, there are three levels, and it helps to know which one you are on. The most basic, the vanilla one, is giving an object a latitude and longitude property or a GeoJSON. That is enough to draw its address, or the shape of a region, a district, a country or a continent, and you can have several shapes attached, even on an object related to yours. If that shape or address changes over time, that can be modelled too, and there is a lot of room to play. In the [documentation](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/geospatial/ontology/) they are called geopoint and geoshape, and objects that have them are indexed for geospatial search.

The second level is geotemporal series: attaching a track to an object, that is, points in space and time. Think of a delivery truck with a GPS reporting its position every few seconds. Those points don’t live in an ordinary property but in a special structure linked to the object, and the [documentation](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/geospatial/geotemporal-series-overview/) (which currently marks it as beta) describes a sync that indexes the data into an optimised database and an object type that references it. It has a lot more depth than it looks. In our experience, how you configure the series depends on what you are going to ask of it: finding which tracks cross a polygon in a time window is not the same as asking where this truck was at five in the afternoon, and with many objects you notice. Underneath there is serious mathematics: we were lucky enough to meet at Palantir the person who built the geo engine, and the technology underneath is, at heart, their doctoral thesis, work on optimising mathematical curves to represent a many-dimensional space in a single dimension. It is delicate ground and it has changed a lot. We started when you had to configure it by hand with a YAML specification file, and today it is far better integrated, with [data modelling](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/geospatial/data-modeling/) and [integration with the ontology](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/geospatial/integrate-geotemporal-series-with-the-ontology/) much better described.

The third level is the geohash, and it will probably ring a bell if you come from doing geo operations in big data. Geo operations are very expensive: comparing distances scales badly with large volumes, and you have to change your mindset so that geo comparisons become problems that parallelise as much as possible, built on spatial indexes. That is where tessellating space comes in. Uber developed [H3](https://h3geo.org/), which indexes space into hexagons of different sizes, and it is the one Palantir has adopted fairly natively: Pipeline Builder includes [geospatial transforms](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/pipeline-builder/transforms-geospatial/) with H3, such as covering a polygon with hexagons or getting a hexagon’s neighbours. You may already do it another way, with ESRI, with [Apache Sedona](https://sedona.apache.org/) or other big-data geo libraries, or even with geographic embeddings, and that can stay. But when we need a fast spatial join, or a use case that checks whether several things are in the same place at the same time, H3 has given us better performance. The idea we take to every project is to think of geo problems as big-data grouping problems: a group by, a window, an operation that can be parallelised over a geospatial index. With that in mind, we start working with you.
